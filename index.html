What you can do with it

Move the year control anywhere from roughly 123,000 BCE to 2019 CE and watch the whole world's political map redraw itself — empires expanding and collapsing, colonial borders shifting, new nations appearing after independence and decolonization. Click any region to see its name, which power it was subject to, roughly how long it existed, and — for hundreds of well-documented countries — real recorded history: predecessor and successor states, and day-precise boundary-change dates sourced from community-mapped historical records.

Want to show it to a class or share a specific moment? Every view has its own link — jump to 1815 after the Congress of Vienna, 1945 after WWII, or 1960 in the middle of African decolonization, and the URL captures exactly that year. There's also a built-in tool to record a play-through across any range of years as a downloadable video, for dropping straight into a lesson or a presentation.

Built for the classroom, not just for fun

This started as a personal passion project and grew into something meant to hold up as an actual teaching resource: no account walls, no ads, no dark patterns — just a map. Every data source is documented, every known limitation is written down rather than hidden, and the boundary data itself comes from a combination of academic and community-maintained historical GIS datasets, cross-checked against each other. Read the full methodology and data sources before citing anything here in serious research — it's a genuinely useful map, not a peer-reviewed atlas.

Frequently asked questions

What did the world's borders look like in 1914, before World War I?

A world of empires: Austria-Hungary still intact, the Ottoman Empire still holding much of the Middle East, and most of Africa carved up between European colonial powers. See 1914 on the map →

How did Africa's borders change after decolonization?

Most of it happened fast, concentrated around 1960 — often called the "Year of Africa," when 17 countries gained independence in a single year. See 1960 on the map →, then drag the slider forward to watch the rest of the continent's borders settle through the 1960s-80s.

Is there a free interactive map that shows how country borders changed throughout history?

Yes — that's exactly what this is. No account, no paywall, and no time limit on how long you can explore. Open the map →

Can I download a video of the map animating through history?

Yes. The map has a built-in "Make a video" tool — pick a start year, an end year, and whether to show labels, and it records a play-through you can download or open in a new tab. Useful for dropping straight into a lesson slide or a presentation without screen-recording software.

How far back in time does this map go?

Roughly 123,000 BCE to 2019 CE — from early human migration patterns through to the present-day political map, combining data from historical-basemaps, CShapes 2.0, Wikidata, and OpenHistoricalMap. See the full methodology for exactly which source covers which years.

Is this accurate enough to cite in a history paper or use in class?

It's genuinely useful and the data sourcing is fully transparent — but it's not a peer-reviewed academic atlas. Every known limitation and approximation is documented on the methodology page; verify anything load-bearing against a primary source, same as you would with any teaching aid.

What makes this different from other historical map tools?

It's a genuine whole-world view rather than a single-region focus, it spans further back into prehistory than most historical atlases (which typically start around 2000-4000 BCE), it has a free video-export tool built in, and for hundreds of well-documented countries it shows real, day-precise boundary-change dates rather than just a year.
